The brief

What we were actually handed

Human mobility is a central goal for happiness and quality of life. Yet, mobility-impaired individuals have few options with respect to wearable assistive technologies that enable movement during the widely-varying scenarios that are encountered during daily life. The Dephy ExoBoot is an ankle exoskeleton designed to assist users when walking or running, but currently there is little control for the user to personalize their experience and comfort while using the ExoBoot… The project will explore different online learning methods to incorporate user preference when using the ExoBoot, and to extend these methods across variable ambulation scenarios that are encountered during daily life. Slide 3, Topic #2 — problem statement, abridged

Underneath the framing, this is a search problem with a human in the loop. The boot applies one torque pulse per stride, and that pulse is defined by about four numbers.

Question one · well-trodden

Learning a preference online

A person can reliably tell you "A feels better than B." They cannot tell you "my optimum is 22.4 N·m at 53% stride." So: search a continuous space from noisy pairwise feedback, in few enough trials that someone will actually sit through it. There is real prior art here — we're building on it, not inventing it.

Question two · genuinely open

Carrying it across contexts

A preference learned on a treadmill at 1.2 m/s is one point in a much larger space. Daily life is ramps, stairs, sit-to-stand, speed changes, jogging. Do you re-run the whole optimization every time the terrain changes — or can a context-conditioned model warm-start the next one?

Reading

What to read, in what order

Read the first three properly, before the first real lab meeting. Skim the rest by week four. Turning up having actually read Lee 2023 is the difference between a vague proposal and a good one.

Tier oneread properly
  1. 01
    User preference optimization for control of ankle exoskeletons using sample efficient active learning
    Lee, Shetty, Franks, Tan, Evangelopoulos, Ha & Rouse · Science Robotics 8(83) · 2023

    The closest published ancestor of our project. Ankle exoskeleton, preference-only feedback, real time. An evolutionary algorithm proposes candidate parameter sets; a RankNet pretrained on earlier human preference data ranks them. It converged in 43 ± 7 queries at 88% accuracy against randomly generated parameters. The press framing — tuning an exoskeleton like tuning a radio station — is worth stealing for our poster.

    The data is public. The Dryad set (doi:10.5061/dryad.p5hqbzktp, ~712 KB, freely licensed) holds 13 subjects × 3 trials of real forced-choice comparisons at 1.2 m/s, in Excel with a README. We can do genuine analysis on this in week one — no hardware, no IRB. This is the highest-leverage item on the whole page.

  2. 02
    Human-in-the-loop optimization of exoskeleton assistance during walking
    Zhang, Fiers, Witte, Jackson, Poggensee, Atkeson & Collins · Science 356:1280–1284 · 2017

    The foundational HIL paper. CMA-ES over a four-parameter ankle torque profile, with metabolic rate from indirect calorimetry as the objective. It got a 24.2 ± 7.4% metabolic reduction — but took over an hour per participant. Read it to understand why preference-based methods exist at all: metabolic measurement is slow, noisy, expensive, tethered, and, crucially, not what the user actually wants. Our project is a reaction to this paper's limitations.

  3. 03
    Uncertainty-Aware Ankle Exoskeleton Control
    Tourk, Galoaa, Shajan, Everett, Young & Shepherd · arXiv:2508.21221 · 2025

    Runs directly on the Dephy ExoBoot EB60. An ensemble of seven temporal convolutional networks estimates gait phase; a parallel estimator flags incoming sensor data as in- or out-of-distribution. Familiar movement gets assistive torque; novel movement drops to zero for safety. Trained on treadmill walking and jogging at varied speeds and inclines, then tested online across ramps, stairs, jumping, sit-to-stand and plyometrics — 97.7% F1 offline, 89.2% online on a new user.

    This is the state of the art on our "variable ambulation" half. Note carefully what it does not do: it handles novelty by refusing to help. That refusal is the opening.

Tier twoskim for method
  1. 04
    The role of user preference in the customized control of robotic exoskeletons
    Ingraham, Remy & Rouse · Science Robotics · 2022

    Why preference is a legitimate objective at all, and where it diverges from metabolic cost. One usefully concrete finding for experiment design: at faster walking speeds, people are more precise at identifying their preferred assistance magnitude.

  2. 05
    Preference-Based Learning for Exoskeleton Gait Optimization · and the high-dimensional follow-up
    Tucker et al. · arXiv:1909.12316 and arXiv:2003.06495 · 2019–2020

    Introduces CoSpar, which asks for pairwise preferences and coactive suggestions ("make it more like that"). A good model of designing the algorithm around what humans can actually report.

  3. 06
    Rapid Online Learning of Hip Exoskeleton Assistance Preferences
    arXiv:2502.15366 · 2025

    Hip rather than ankle, but a clean protocol template. Bayesian preference learning by Metropolis–Hastings via the APReL library, over six torque features, with a linear reward and a soft-max response model. Just 12 pairwise comparisons per session: 20 s on A, 5 s rest, 20 s on B, then say which. Its own stated limitations — one walking speed, linear reward, no energetics — are literally our opening.

  4. 07
    Personalizing exoskeleton assistance while walking in the real world
    Slade, Kochenderfer, Delp & Collins · Nature · 2022

    Data-driven optimization outdoors using wearable sensors instead of a metabolic cart — as effective as the lab method and four times faster. The "get out of the lab" argument, and squarely aligned with what Finn works on.

  5. 08
    AI-driven universal lower-limb exoskeleton system for community ambulation
    Science Advances

    Switches assistance type between locomotion modes and modulates by ground slope, user-independent, with no separate mode classifier. Read it as a foil: it generalizes across contexts but not across people.

The opening

Where the actual opportunity is

Put the literature on two axes and the shape of the gap becomes obvious. One body of work personalizes beautifully but in a single context. Another generalizes across contexts but treats every wearer the same. Almost nothing occupies the top right.

Nobody has cleanly solved the transfer problem: how do you carry a person's learned preference across contexts without re-running the full procedure every time the terrain changes? At roughly 40 queries per context and six daily-life ambulation modes, that's 240 queries. Nobody will sit through that. Any result that meaningfully cuts the number is a real contribution — and it's narrow enough to actually finish in one semester.

Groundwork

What to shore up

Be honest about which of these you already have. The gaps are what September is for.

Biomechanics

  • The gait cycle: stance and swing, heel strike, mid-stance, push-off, toe-off. Everything is parameterized as % of stride.
  • Ankle kinematics — plantarflexion vs. dorsiflexion, which muscles the boot offloads (soleus, gastrocnemius), and why push-off is where the energy is.
  • Gait phase estimation: the core real-time problem. Turning a noisy IMU and encoder stream into "we are 47% through this stride" so torque lands on time.
  • Why modes genuinely differ — stairs, ramps and level ground have different ankle moment profiles, not scaled versions of one.

Learning & optimization

  • Bayesian optimization and Gaussian processes — the standard tool for expensive, noisy, low-dimensional black-box objectives. The mathematical heart of the project.
  • Preference and dueling models: Bradley–Terry, soft-max response models, RankNet. Building a utility function out of ordinal comparisons.
  • Active learning and acquisition functions — how the algorithm picks the next pair to show. "Sample efficient" is doing real work in that title; every wasted query costs 45 seconds of someone's patience.
  • CMA-ES and evolution strategies. Contextual bandits, transfer and warm-starting for part two.
  • Why human feedback is hard: noisy, non-stationary, order-dependent, subject to habituation. Design the protocol assuming all of it.

Engineering practice

  • Python: numpy, scipy, pandas, matplotlib, scikit-learn. PyTorch only once we reach RankNet-shaped work.
  • Real-time loops — sampling rates, latency, and why a fast control loop can't wait on a slow Python callback.
  • Reading hardware sensor streams; logging and timestamping data we can actually analyze afterwards.
  • Git from day one. Three people, one codebase.
Logistics

The parts that quietly block people

Human subjects training

This is human-subjects research. We'll likely need CITI human subjects research training before any of us can be listed on a protocol or run participants. The certification takes a few hours; the approval paperwork has lead time on top. Ask Finn in the first meeting — this is the single most common thing that silently stalls first-years for a month.

Whatever the SAHM Lab space in AHG requires. Ask early, do it early.

You will be a pilot participant

We'll almost certainly wear the boot ourselves. Expect it — and pay attention to what it actually feels like. That intuition is genuinely useful when designing a preference interface.

First meeting

Questions to bring to Finn

Specific questions signal we did the reading. These are ordered so the early ones scope the work.

Scope

  1. Are we building on the Lee et al. RankNet-plus-evolutionary approach, or exploring Bayesian optimization and CoSpar-style methods as alternatives?
  2. Is the deliverable a working real-time controller on the boot, or an offline study using existing preference data? What does success look like by December?
  3. Which torque parameters are we tuning — the standard four, or something else?

Ambulation scenarios

  1. Which scenarios are actually in scope? Treadmill speeds only, or ramps and stairs too?
  2. For generalization: context-conditioned models, transfer and warm-starting, or per-context re-optimization as the baseline?

Practicalities

  1. Do we need CITI human subjects training, and when should we start it?
  2. Which ExoBoot model does the lab have, and what's the existing codebase — opensourceleg, flexsea directly, or a lab fork?
  3. Is there existing SAHM Lab preference data we can analyze while we get up to speed on the hardware?
  4. How would you split work across three of us? One option: hardware and control, learning algorithm, experimental protocol and analysis — with everyone reading everything.

Framing

  1. Who is the intended user population — people with mobility impairment, older adults, unimpaired augmentation? It changes what "preference" even means.
